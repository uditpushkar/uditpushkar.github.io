## The Space Where Life Actually Happens

Martin Crowe, the great New Zealand batsman, wrote about **Mind the Gap**. Not as a railway announcement, but as a way of looking at life and performance. The phrase has stayed with me because it is simple enough to be ignored and deep enough to be useful. The gap is not empty. The gap is where the game changes. <!--more-->

In cricket, it is the gap between seeing the ball and committing the shot. In life, it is the gap between anger and reply, ambition and action, hunger and discipline, breath and breath. We keep thinking life is lived in grand declarations. It is not. Life is lived in the tiny pause before we do something stupid, or something brave, or something boring that compounds over time.

The successful person is not always the most talented person. Often, he is simply the person who can live inside the gap without panicking. He can wait. He can observe. He can delay the cheap reaction. He can breathe before swinging.

Happiness also lives there. Health lives there. Decency lives there. The gap between two breaths is not dramatic, so we do not respect it. But if you cannot find yourself there, you will not find yourself in the promotion, the new city, the better salary, the fancy watch, or the airport lounge. The mind will simply carry its mess into a better-lit room.

Japan made me think about this gap again.

## Japan Feels Like Somebody Cares

When I travelled through Japan, I had a strange feeling while doing the most ordinary things. Walking on a street. Waiting at a crossing. Standing near a curb. Listening to the small electronic sound that tells pedestrians it is safe to cross.

It felt like somebody cared.

Not in a sentimental greeting-card way. Not "people are so nice" tourism brochure nonsense. I mean something more structural. The height of the curb felt thought through. The streets were clean without looking aggressively policed. The crossings had sound. The trains arrived like time itself had put on a uniform. The public toilets were not a test of one’s spiritual strength. The city did not constantly ask you to adjust, tolerate, manage, bargain, avoid, duck, improvise.

Japan’s care is not loud. It is embedded. It is in the process. It is in the detail. It is in the assumption that an ordinary citizen deserves a functional day.

That is a radical idea if you come from India.

In India, public life often feels like a negotiation with neglect. Pavement exists, then disappears, then becomes parking, then becomes a shop, then becomes rubble, then becomes philosophy. A road is built, dug, repaired, dug again, forgotten, inaugurated, and then swallowed by dust. The citizen is expected to be resilient. We praise resilience because it is cheaper than competence.

Japan ruined me for good.

After Japan, nothing impresses me easily in infrastructure, transportation, processes, or government projects. The bar has shifted. A flyover no longer makes me emotional. A metro announcement does not make me clap. A clean platform is not a miracle. It is a baseline. Japan shows you what happens when a society takes the boring things seriously for decades.

And once you have seen that, you cannot unsee it.

## GDP Is Not Civilization

India has now overtaken Japan in headline GDP numbers. Very good. Everyone can distribute laddoos. But GDP is a strange animal. It can make a country look powerful on paper while its citizens are still fighting with potholes, paperwork, low wages, weak skills, poor service delivery, and a daily assault of friction.

There is a famous joke attributed to Elon Musk about economists and GDP: if people start eating shit and charging each other for it, GDP goes up. Crude, yes. Also useful. The joke works because it exposes something important. Calculation is not civilization. A bigger number is not automatically a better life.

Japan’s economy has its own problems: ageing population, slow growth, debt, deflationary habits, too much caution. But the lived experience of Japan remains extraordinary because productivity, trust, process, training, public discipline, and urban order have been built into daily life.

India, meanwhile, remains painfully unproductive in large parts of the economy. Too many people are trapped in informal work. Too many businesses remain tiny not because they are charming but because they are inefficient. Too many workers accept Rs 10,000 a month because the economy has trained them to expect little and demand less.

How will we grow if wages remain low, skills remain thin, and informality remains the national operating system?

We keep celebrating employment without asking what kind of employment it is. Food delivery and quick commerce jobs are presented as urban opportunity. In reality, many of these are bad jobs wrapped in app design. The worker gets a bag, a target, a rating, a penalty, and a polite notification that can end his livelihood. There is no deep skill formation. No serious apprenticeship. No ladder. No meaningful protection. No dignity beyond the motivational poster.

He learns traffic shortcuts, not a trade. He learns algorithmic obedience, not bargaining power. He learns how to survive rain, heat, angry customers, parking guards and app glitches. That is effort, no doubt. But effort without upward mobility is not development. It is exhaustion with branding.

An economy becomes rich when people become more productive, and people become more productive when systems help them learn, earn, organize, specialize and demand better. India cannot become a developed country on cheap labour, jugaad, and a billion people waiting for discount coupons.

The gap between India and Japan is not merely income. It is process. It is reliability. It is the expectation of care.

## The Tennis Lesson

Tennis has been teaching me the same lesson in a humiliatingly physical way.

I have been playing for around three months. There has been improvement. Real improvement. The ball now occasionally goes where I intend, which feels like witchcraft. I can rally a little. I understand more. I move better. I have discovered that the body has many joints and all of them can file complaints independently.

And yet, I am still a noob.

This is the funny cruelty of tennis. It takes monumental effort just to become mid. Not good. Not tournament-level. Not stylish Instagram slow-motion forehand. Just mid. Respectably average. The kind of player who does not donate every third ball to the parking lot.

Tennis looks elegant from far away because television hides the violence of competence. The timing, footwork, grip, spacing, split step, recovery, shoulder rotation, follow-through, serve toss, and mental reset all have to arrive together like a disciplined cabinet meeting. If one minister is absent, the ball goes into the net.

The gap in tennis is literal. Too close to the ball, disaster. Too far, disaster. Swing too early, disaster. Too late, disaster. Hit without breathing, disaster. Think too much, disaster. Do not think at all, also disaster. Tennis is a sport invented to expose impatience.

It is also addictive because progress is visible but never generous. It gives you one good shot, then immediately reminds you not to get carried away. It is a beautiful dictatorship.

## Mind It Properly

So maybe the phrase works everywhere.

Mind the gap between breath and reaction.

Mind the gap between a clean Japanese street and an Indian one where the citizen has been trained to lower expectations.

Mind the gap between GDP headlines and the actual quality of work.

Mind the gap between playing tennis for three months and pretending you are one coaching tip away from Federer.

Most of life is gap management. The gap between what we say and what we do. The gap between what a country claims and what a citizen feels. The gap between effort and skill. The gap between noise and care.

If India wants to become serious, it must mind the gap. Not with slogans. With wages, skills, processes, public design, cleaner streets, safer work, better training, and systems that make ordinary life less tiring.

If I want to become better, I must mind my own smaller gaps too. The breath before the reply. The pause before the shot. The decision to show up again even when being bad at something is deeply inconvenient to the ego.

The gap is where decline begins. It is also where improvement starts.

The trick is to notice it before it becomes a canyon.
