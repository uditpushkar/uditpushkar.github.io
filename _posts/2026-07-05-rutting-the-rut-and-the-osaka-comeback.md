## Getting Out

There are days when the rut does not arrive like a dramatic villain. It arrives like a blanket. It is soft at first. Almost comforting. Then suddenly it is a little suffocating. One inconsistent workout becomes three. One indulgent meal becomes a festival. A few drinks become more than a few. Instagram becomes archaeology. You scroll through reels as if somewhere between one dog video and one tennis highlight, the meaning of life is hiding with a discount code. <!--more-->

The last few days have been holidays for me. Which is a polite way of saying that structure quietly left the room. Exercise became inconsistent. Food became excessive. Drinks appeared with more confidence than required. And the phone, that small glowing dictator, did what it does best: it kept offering me one more thing.

Why do we get into a rut?

Because it is easy. Because the mind loves low resistance. Modern life has also become very good at giving us comfort without real recovery. It gives stimulation, but not nourishment. Entertainment, but not rest. Earlier, even laziness had limits. You could sit, stare at the fan, maybe read something, maybe sleep. Now laziness has infinite scroll. It has autoplay. It has reels and shorts and ten strangers explaining productivity while you actively avoid being productive.

We are more prone to ruts now because the rut has become engineered. It is not just weakness. It is design. The phone does not merely distract. It trains the mind to prefer interruption. And once the mind begins to prefer interruption, discipline feels like physical labour.

Which, unfortunately, it is.

## The Long Way Back

Over the last few months, I have entered and exited this rut enough times to stop treating it like a moral emergency. Earlier, I would get angry at myself. Then I would overcorrect. Then I would fail at that overcorrection. Then I would become angrier. It was a very efficient system, if the objective was to produce guilt and no results.

Now I understand it slightly better. Getting out of a rut does not require panic. It requires a pattern break.

For me, that pattern break is long endurance.

Today, I went for a 20 km cycle ride in a wet and windy evening. The sky looked undecided. The wind behaved as if it had personal issues with me. Most of the ride was against the wind. The road was wet. The air was heavy. The effort was honest.

My cycle had recently come back from service at Decathlon, though "service" may be a generous word. Too much grease had been applied, and somehow the sanctity of the seat had been disturbed. A bicycle seat has very few responsibilities in life. It should stay put, not become a philosophical problem. But we moved on. We endured. Civilization survived.

And somewhere in that ride, the mind began to clear.

This is what endurance does. It forces the mind to stop jumping. You cannot keep doom-scrolling mentally while riding against the wind for an hour. The body asks for attention. The road asks for attention. The breath too. The wind keeps giving its rude reminders. Slowly, the thinking pattern breaks.

That is the trick. Not motivation. Not a grand declaration. Not a new notebook. Just a long enough activity that forces you to stay with one thing until the mind stops behaving like a monkey with Wi-Fi.

## Tours and the Danger of Losing the Thread

From 7th to 15th July, I will be on tour to Assam. This will be my fourth, or maybe fifth, trip to Assam with the Governor. Guwahati again. Assam again. The familiar machinery of movement will start again. Packing. Protocol. Travel. Hotels. Schedules. Calls. Files. And that strange floating feeling of being away from your own routine.

Tours are useful. Tours are also dangerous.

Not dangerous in the dramatic sense. More in the slow leak sense. Whenever I travel for work, I lose contact with the small systems that hold my life together. Exercise disappears first. Nutrition becomes negotiable. Writing becomes "I will do it tomorrow." The wellness app work gets pushed aside. Calls home become shorter. The garden becomes a photograph in the mind. By the time the tour ends, the thread is loose.

This time I want to be mindful.

I want to exercise there, even if it is basic. I want to eat with some sense. I want to keep the blogging alive. I want to keep working on the wellness app. I want to stay connected to home, family, and the little domestic orbit that gives life its shape. The point is not to maintain perfection on tour. That is nonsense. The point is to not abandon the thread completely.

Because rutting begins exactly there. One abandoned thread. Then another. Then another. Then suddenly you are not living; you are just restarting.

## The System and the Friction

It has been two months since Japan, and I still keep thinking about governance.

Japan makes you notice friction. Or rather, it makes you notice the absence of it. The street works. The crossing works. The transport works. The public system seems to have accepted one basic idea: ordinary people should not have to fight the environment every day.

India is different. Of course it is different. India is huge. It is messy. It is federal and argumentative and administratively overburdened. We have 160 crore people and more than two dozen states. Each state brings its own history and politics. Governance here is not a clean laboratory experiment.

But even after accepting all that, one thing remains true: many inefficiencies can be fixed.

Not all. Many.

And often, fixing them does not require genius. It requires attention. It requires somebody to care enough about the small broken process. It requires someone to say: this is not good enough. That sentence is harder than it looks because nobody likes being told their work is not good enough. It is natural. People have their own pressures and blind spots. But there must be some common objective standard. Some basic agreement that public systems should work better than they currently do.

I cannot write exact professional examples here, and perhaps I should not. But I can say this much: institutions are getting weaker. And the worrying part is not merely that they are weakening. The worrying part is that many people do not seem alarmed by it.

The rut is not only personal. Institutions also get into ruts. Offices get into ruts. Systems get into ruts. Files and habits become the endless scroll of governance. Hierarchy adds its own weight. Excuses become procedure.

And like the individual, the institution also needs a pattern break.

## Garden Logic

At home, the garden has been doing well.

The rain over the last two to three days has helped. More than 24 hours of rain is no small blessing for new planting. The timing turned out right. The plants have moisture now, and hopefully they will grow with vigour.

I have also started taking more practical interest in bonsai. Earlier, it was mostly admiration. Now I have actually begun doing it with my hands. I have made one bonsai tree, and hopefully someday I will post its picture.

There is something deeply peaceful about watching plants work. They do not make announcements. They do not post updates. They do not say, "New journey begins." They simply grow. A little every day.

Plants are rude teachers because they make your excuses look childish. They remind you that growth does not need drama. It needs continuity. It needs water, light, pruning, patience and attention. Mostly, it needs showing up.

Maybe this is why the garden feels healing. It is the opposite of the rut. The rut is repetition without growth. The garden is repetition with growth.

## Tennis and the Osaka Spark

The other thing that has taken hold of me is tennis.

I have been playing for more than three months now. There has been coaching. There have been drills. There has been footwork and repeated humiliation. Some improvement too. My backhand remains shaky. Poor, frankly. It behaves like a government file looking for the correct table. My forehand, however, has started working better. Not great. Not elegant. But occasionally respectable, which in tennis is a major emotional event.

I have also started watching tennis with real interest. Tonight I was watching Naomi Osaka against Aryna Sabalenka in the Wimbledon fourth round. Sabalenka was the world number one and top seed. Osaka came in with her history, her talent, motherhood, comeback, and that strange calm intensity she has.

And then Osaka won.

She beat Sabalenka 6-2, 7-6. A proper upset. A proper statement. Just like that, she became my favourite female athlete.

Maybe I am sentimental because I watched it on a day when I was trying to get myself back on track. But comebacks hit differently when you are also trying one, however small.

Osaka's comeback is not my cycling ride, obviously. One is elite sport under global attention. The other is one man riding a slightly over-greased cycle through wind and rain. But the emotional grammar is similar. You fall out. You lose rhythm. You doubt the old level. You return anyway. You endure the awkward middle. Then one day, something clicks.

That is the lesson.

The rut is not escaped by self-hatred. It is escaped by movement. By endurance. By returning to the court and the road. By returning to the garden, the page, the work, the family. By returning to the thing you know quietly matters.

The mind does not always need a lecture.

Sometimes it needs 20 km against the wind.
